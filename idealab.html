Market structure, macro, ensembles: round 11 (Oct 22)

197 more ideas against v1.7 on 2000–26, 2010–26 and the unseen 1988–99. They covered Kritzman turbulence and absorption ratio, breadth, skew, variance ratio, 52-week highs, overextension, O'Neil follow-through days, capitulation volume, Coppock, Supertrend, ADX, Aroon, Keltner, Bollinger, NR7, Gayed's utilities signal, copper, emerging markets, financials, tech leadership, stock/bond correlation, Fed hiking, VIX level, shock filters, 5-signal votes, a Fed-day swap of 9Sig's reserve, sleeve risk-parity and momentum splits, and trading the account's own equity curve.

No adoption. Three passed on paper; none survived the neighbour tests. The earnings-season boost (+1.5 pt) needs its exact dates: shifting the window a few days gives −1.6 to +0.1, and the drawdown worsens to −45 to −57%. Overextension caps add 0.5 pt at most. The most interesting was a weekly RSI > 70 cap, worth +2.1 pt with an equal drawdown in 2000–26 but −1.5 in 1988–99. Over a threshold grid from 60 to 85 its monthly t-stat stays between 0.2 and 0.9, so it goes on the watchlist only. A Fed-day swap of the reserve into SOXL adds about +1.2 pt but lifts the odds of a >50% drop from 15% to 22%: more risk, not an edge. Equity-curve rules on the account all cost return. Correction: round 10's "overlay off when S&P 2-year return < 0" never fired (data window too short); re-run properly, it's +0.1 pt.

Famous traders and quant signals: round 10 (Oct 22)

99 new ideas against v1.7, scored on 2000–26, 2010–26 and the unseen 1988–99: O'Neil distribution days, Minervini volatility contraction and trend template, Raschke Holy Grail, Larry Williams %R, Dow Theory, Zweig "don't fight the Fed" and 4% rule, Weinstein stage 2, Seykota/Donchian, Dennis/Darvas breakouts, Kaufman efficiency, LeBeau chandelier stop, Ichimoku, Elder weekly MACD, copper/gold, the dollar, small-cap and semiconductor leadership, correlation spikes, FOMC even weeks, bond carry and momentum-crash protection. Each signal was tried as a cap on TQQQ when it's off, a full-TQQQ boost when it's on, and a split shift.

No adoption. Two passed on the risk rule, by tiny margins: a Williams %R < −90 dip boost (+0.2 / +0.4 / +0.4 pt), but −80 and −85 push the 2000–26 drawdown to −39–41% and it was better in only 7 of 38 years; and a volatility-contraction boost (about +0.0). Caps and exits cost 2–12 points a year because TQQQ's best days come in sharp rebounds; boosts on regime signals produced −55% to −79% drawdowns; breakout and Donchian rules all lost to the current dual trend. The classic methods mostly duplicate what v1.7 already has (a trend filter, a volatility switch, a dip buyer). In the table below, round 10's "Windows" column shows the 1988–99 change.

Is dual trend overfit? Round 9 (Oct 22)

Five checks on v1.7's dual trend: it helped on 1988–1999 data never used in design (+0.7 pt); every fast average from 20 to 40 days helped in all three periods; picked year by year from past data only it still added +1.3 pt; monthly t-stat 1.85, better in 23 of 38 years; and it improved all 6 standard trend portfolios tested (Faber 5-asset max drawdown −16.3% → −11.6%). Verdict: a real but modest effect; expect about +1 pt a year live.

Round 9 then tested 96 ideas against v1.7 on 2000–26, 2010–26 and the unseen 1988–99. Three passed on paper; none survived follow-up: "LDS core = top 2 by momentum" (+0.6 / +1.7 / +1.4) loses most of its gain with other momentum windows and was better in only 21 of 38 years (watchlist); a 0.5% trend hysteresis turns negative at 1–2%; the Santa rally depends on its start date. The whole strategy earned 34.1% a year in 1988–99, but with a −47% drawdown: simulations on 1988–2026 put the odds of a >50% drop near 15%. Reducing reliance on tech (round 7, Oct 21)

Mixing non-tech 3x ETFs (UPRO, UDOW, TNA, CURE, FAS, ERX) into 9Sig's growth engine was worse when the volatility switch read the mix: through the 2000–02 crash −28% to −63% instead of −4.8%, because TQQQ's volatility is the early warning that moves 9Sig to bonds and gold. Keeping the switch on TQQQ while holding a mix costs 1.5–3 points a year (TQQQ 75/UPRO 25: 34.8%; 50/50: 33.3%) for a similar drawdown, but halves Nasdaq concentration. Its payoff would only come in a slow, crash-free tech slump, which the history doesn't contain. Decision: keep TQQQ only.

Which number to watch

  • CAGR from 2010 (real ETFs only). The most honest return number. Earlier years use rebuilt ETF prices.
  • Martin ratio (CAGR ÷ Ulcer index). The Ulcer index counts how deep and how long you are below your last high, so this is return per unit of pain. v1.6: ${D2.base6.martin} (2000–26), ${D2.base6.martin10} (2010–26).
  • Max drawdown and longest time underwater. Whether you can keep placing the orders.
  • Worst 10-year CAGR. Your result if you started at the worst possible time (v1.6: 27.8%).

On a 40–50% CAGR goal: nothing that fits a Roth IRA with 3x ETFs got there. The frontier is about 36–40% with a 37–45% drawdown (see the 70/30 row). The ideas that reached 41–48% in Idea Lab I needed futures leverage and had 53–59% drawdowns, plus a large risk on a 1987-style day. Futures in an IRA need $25k+ and margin approval, and contract sizes fit accounts of $150k and up.

Why the backtest drawdown is smaller than SPY's

The volatility switch shrinks TQQQ as markets get choppy, and LDS only holds assets above their 210-day average, so in slow bear markets the account moves into Treasuries and gold. In 2008 v1.6 rose 58% while SPY fell 55%. In 2022 and Q4 2018 it lost more than SPY, because stocks and bonds fell together. The −37% is one historical path. Plan for a 40–50% drop at some point; the dashboard's Backtest tab has the full detail.

Year by year

What didn't work

  • Almost every single parameter tweak landed within ±0.5 points of the baseline. The engine sits near a local optimum.
  • Implied volatility (VIX) for the volatility switch: −12 points CAGR, −92% drawdown. Realized volatility is much better here.
  • Swapping LDS into TQQQ on turn-of-month or pre-holiday days: drawdowns of −73%.
  • Parking de-risked 9Sig money in QQQ or QLD instead of bonds and gold: drawdowns of −73% to −86%.
  • More LDS assets (emerging markets, small caps, 7–10yr Treasuries, tech): lower CAGR. Utilities added CAGR but a −51% drawdown from 2007.
  • Holding the Fed swap 2–3 days before or a day after the decision: worse on both counts.
  • Different 50/50 reset schedules, tilts and splits: nothing robust. 60/40 adds 1.2 points but a −46% drawdown from 2007.

All 155 ideas

Pass = CAGR at least +0.5 pt, max drawdown no more than 3 pt worse (2000–26 and 2007–26), ahead in at least 12 of 17 ten-year windows, and ahead in both halves. Rules were set before any results came in.

How this was tested

  • Engine: the production twin_engine.py daily step, replayed close by close. On identical prices the harness and the live engine end within $0 of each other over 3 years, and the idea-switch copy matches the engine within 0.06% over 26 years.
  • Prices: real ETFs where they exist. Before launch, leveraged ETFs are rebuilt from the index (QQQ ← Nasdaq-100, SPY ← S&P 500, TLT ← Vanguard long Treasury fund, SOXL ← PHLX semiconductor index) with T-bill borrowing cost, fees and a drag calibrated to match each real ETF (daily correlation 0.98–0.999).
  • Gold before Aug 2000 uses the gold-miners index scaled to gold's volatility. It affects about 18 months of the LDS gold slice.
  • The baseline here (30.6% / −52%) is below the earlier chat's 33.8% / −43.6% because of data differences in the 2000–01 crash. From 2007 the two agree within a point. Comparisons on this page all use the same data.
  • 155 configurations were tried. With that many tries some winners are luck, which is why the finalist had to pass stress tests and the 2012 walk-forward check as well.
Backtests, not promises. Leveraged ETFs can lose most of their value quickly. Not financial advice.
